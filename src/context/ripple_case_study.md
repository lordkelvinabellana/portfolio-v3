# Case Study — Ripple

---

# Project Name

## Ripple

Cloud Invoicing + Financial Visibility Platform

---

# Problem

Ripple was built to help users manage cloud invoicing, financial visibility, and cost accountability across multi-cloud environments.

The product sat close to both finance and engineering, which meant delivery problems were never only technical.

They were usually a combination of:

- product clarity
- delivery speed
- stakeholder alignment
- UI/UX readiness
- architecture limitations
- customer success needs
- business urgency

As the product grew, one major issue became clear:

feature delivery was slowing down.

Some features were blocked by missing design support.

Engineering priorities were spread across multiple connected products.

And the existing monolithic structure made release cycles heavier than necessary.

The challenge was not simply building features.

The challenge was protecting product delivery.

---

# Why It Mattered

Products tied to invoicing and financial workflows require trust.

Users rely on the platform for financial decisions, reporting confidence, and operational clarity.

Delays, unclear UX, or slow delivery create business risk quickly.

Internally, delivery speed mattered because Ripple supported multiple connected tools and customer-facing workflows.

If engineering slowed down, customer success slowed down.

If product decisions were unclear, release quality suffered.

This made product ownership critical.

The job was not only deciding what to build.

It was helping the entire delivery system move.

---

# Solution

I approached Ripple as a delivery system problem.

The goal was not only shipping features.

It was removing the blockers that slowed product movement.

That meant stepping into:

product ownership

design ownership

architecture alignment

stakeholder coordination

wherever delivery required it.

---

# Major Solution Areas

## UI/UX Ownership to Unblock Delivery

One of the strongest examples was stepping into UI/UX responsibilities.

Our main designer was heavily focused on Octo, and Ripple feature delivery started slowing because several features could not move forward without proper design support.

Waiting would have created unnecessary delivery delays.

Instead, I stepped in and handled SaaS UI/UX design using Figma.

This included:

- feature layouts
- user flows
- implementation-ready design references
- maintaining consistency with the existing platform
- helping engineering move faster through clearer implementation paths

This was one of the strongest ownership moments in my career because it directly protected delivery speed.

---

## Product Ownership Across Multiple Systems

Managing Ripple also meant managing connected operational tools and internal systems.

Prioritization became more important than raw output.

Not every feature deserved immediate delivery.

The goal was balancing:

customer value

engineering cost

business urgency

This required constant coordination with:

- engineering teams
- customer success teams
- sales support
- leadership

Good product ownership was often saying:

not now

instead of

yes to everything

That protected quality and team focus.

---

## Ripple V2 — Monolith to Microfrontend Migration

One of the most important product initiatives was the transition from Ripple V1 to Ripple V2.

## V1

Monolithic architecture

## V2

Microfrontend architecture

The old structure slowed delivery because even smaller updates often required broader release coordination.

The new architecture improved:

- modular development
- release speed
- engineering flexibility
- ownership clarity
- long-term maintainability

## My Involvement

- aligned product priorities around migration timing
- helped engineering coordinate feature planning during the transition
- supported delivery planning around the architecture shift
- prioritized features with the new release structure in mind

This was a major product shift because architecture directly affected business speed.

---

## Product Demo + Stakeholder Support

I also created product demo slides and feature presentation materials used by sales and customer-facing teams.

Since many customers were based in Japan, I worked closely with customer success and sales internally rather than directly handling customer-facing calls.

The goal was helping internal teams communicate product value clearly and consistently.

This strengthened alignment between:

what was built

and

how the business explained it

That matters more than most teams realize.

# Business Impact

The biggest result was protecting delivery speed.

This created:

- fewer blocked features
- faster implementation through UI ownership
- stronger prioritization across multiple products
- cleaner alignment between engineering and customer success
- better release flexibility through V2 architecture migration
- stronger product clarity for internal sales and customer teams

Instead of only managing tickets,

the work improved how the entire product organization moved.

That created compounding business value.

---

# Challenges

The hardest part was balancing multiple products without letting priorities become reactive.

When several products compete for urgency, everything starts feeling like top priority.

That is dangerous.

The challenge was protecting focus and making practical prioritization decisions.

Another challenge was stepping into design ownership without formal design ownership.

The responsibility existed before the title did.

That required confidence and speed more than permission.

This taught me that product leadership often starts before the role formally exists.

---

# Key Lessons

Ripple taught me that product ownership is mostly about protecting clarity.

Clear priorities

clear delivery paths

clear stakeholder alignment

Without clarity, even strong teams slow down.

It also taught me that architecture decisions are product decisions.

Moving from monolith to microfrontend was not just an engineering upgrade.

It changed delivery speed, ownership structure, and business flexibility.

That perspective permanently changed how I think about product work:

delivery is a product.

Not just the features inside it.
